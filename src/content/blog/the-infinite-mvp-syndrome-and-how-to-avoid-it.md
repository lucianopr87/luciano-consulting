We've all lived through this project: it keeps growing in scope for months, and every sprint adds something new — one more integration, an alternate flow, an "important" improvement before showing it to the outside world. The problem wasn't the quality of what was being built — it was actually very good. The problem is it still hadn't shipped. No real user had touched it. Scope kept growing exponentially, and the launch date either didn't exist or kept slipping every time "just one more thing" came up. There was this comfortable feeling that the date was negotiable, that every new addition justified itself.

I've seen this pattern repeat under different names and in different contexts, and it always confirms the same thing: projects don't fail because the team picked the wrong framework or database. That happens sometimes, but in my experience it's the least common failure. They fail because nobody commits to a date, and without a date, scope has no ceiling. Every week something comes up that "makes sense" to add before launch, and that launch — the only moment the project actually starts learning from reality — keeps getting pushed back.

**The AI paradox in MVPs**

Here's something counterintuitive worth calling out, especially now: generative AI made building faster than ever, and that should mean shipping sooner, not later. But in practice the opposite happens on many teams — because adding one more feature is now easier and cheaper, teams add more features before launch instead of using that speed to reach the market sooner and adjust with real data. The same tool that should shorten the path to launch ends up lengthening it.

My take here is simple: committing to a launch date isn't reckless — it's the discipline that keeps scope from growing without limit. And that date shouldn't line up with "when it's perfect" — it should line up with the moment you can test your core solution against real users. Anything that isn't necessary for that test can wait.

But committing to a date and launching early isn't enough if you can't read what happened afterward. Testing without metrics defined in advance is just watching and guessing — "seems like they liked it," "they didn't use it much." That's not learning, it's intuition dressed up as data. If you didn't decide what you were going to measure before launch, you'll end up interpreting the results to confirm whatever you already wanted to believe.

How do I put this into practice when I work with a founder?

1. **The date gets set first, scope comes after — not the other way around.** If scope defines the date, there will always be a reason to push it. If the date is fixed, scope gets negotiated against it.
2. **I identify the "core solution" that needs validating**, and anything that doesn't directly contribute to that validation stays out of v1, no exceptions. It doesn't matter how well thought out a feature is — if it's not necessary for the test with real users, it's v2. Of course, trade-offs and small adjustments happen along the way — that's the spirit of agile — but focus has to stay committed to the MVP and the launch date if we want to maximize the odds of success.
3. **We define the metrics before launch, not after.** Two or three concrete numbers that will tell you whether the core solution worked — activation, core-flow completion, week-one retention, whatever fits the case. If you can't measure it, you can't know whether the MVP worked; you can only have an opinion.
4. **I use the speed AI gives us for the right thing: getting to testing sooner, not squeezing in more before testing.** Today you can prototype and adjust a flow in hours, not weeks — that's one more reason to ship fast and learn fast, not to pile on features "while you still can."
5. **Every new feature that comes up during development gets written down, not debated in the moment.** It goes into a v2 backlog. Discussing it there, after launch, with real user data and against the metrics you defined, is a completely different — and better — conversation than debating it blindly before launch.

The question I ask any founder who's unsure whether something belongs in the MVP is always the same: do you need this to know whether your core solution works? If the answer is no, it doesn't matter how much sense it makes — it doesn't go in. And the second question, the one almost nobody asks in time: if this shipped next week, what number would you look at to know if it worked?

A project that keeps growing in features without ever shipping isn't "almost ready." It's avoiding the moment when someone outside the team gets to say whether it works — with data, not opinions. The sooner that happens, the sooner you'll know where to actually invest your time — and today, with how fast you can iterate, there's no excuse not to get there sooner.
